# Winning approvals without breaching chargeback limits

No major payment service provider publishes its actual card authorization rate. Every figure on the market, from Stripe to Fiserv to EBANX, is a self-reported *uplift* measured against a baseline the vendor chooses, and as of October 2026 no independent, audited head-to-head comparison exists. Read together, the claims still form a clear hierarchy. Global PSPs (Stripe, Adyen, Checkout.com, Braintree) advertise **average gains of roughly 1–4%**, legacy acquirers (Worldpay, Fiserv, Cybersource) claim similar single-digit gains backed by issuer partnerships, and orchestration layers add low single digits in their most concrete case studies. Emerging-market specialists (EBANX, Nuvei, dLocal, Razorpay) claim the largest gains, because moving cross-border card traffic onto local acquiring lifts approvals from **roughly 30–60% to 70–90%**. Against vendor-estimated card-not-present (CNP) baselines of 85–95%, the biggest levers are therefore structural: where a merchant acquires and what data the issuer sees, not which top-tier ML engine it buys. Industry best practice for balancing approvals against fraud and chargebacks is a constrained margin optimization. It starts by sending issuers richer data, which Visa and Adyen showed lifts first-attempt approvals by **170–310 bps with stable fraud**, and then sets block thresholds by break-even economics, using step-up authentication for the grey band instead of blunt declines. Throughout, the merchant keeps Visa's count-based VAMP ratio well below the 0.5–0.7% acquirer lines that drive PSP enforcement; since April 2026 that ratio trips at **1.5%** in most regions, and it counts fraud reports even on refunded or authenticated orders.

## Vendor benchmarks put card-not-present approvals near 85–95%

No public benchmark of card approval rates by region, card type or merchant category discloses its methodology. Regulators and the card networks publish fraud rates, not approval rates, so the approval figures in circulation are vendor estimates. Orchestrator Gr4vy says general e-commerce CNP traffic typically authorizes at **85–92%**, well-configured North American domestic traffic at 92–95%, and optimized global merchants at 91–96%, and it cites no source for any of these ([Gr4vy](https://gr4vy.com/posts/top-12-payment-performance-benchmarks-for-2026/)). A benchmark aggregator puts US CNP at 85–90% against **98–99% card-present** ([Payments & Risk](https://paymentsandrisk.com/docs/reference/benchmarks/)), and Fiserv's Carat says CNP approvals "hover around 80+%" versus 95%+ in store ([Fiserv Carat](https://www.carat.fiserv.com/en-us/resources/how-to-optimize-your-commerce-model/)). By the same vendor estimates, cross-border cards authorize **5–15 points below domestic** ones ([Gr4vy](https://gr4vy.com/posts/top-12-payment-performance-benchmarks-for-2026/)).

The choice of definition alone moves these numbers by several points. Visa counts "approved authorizations divided by total authorization attempts based upon first attempt of a unique transaction" ([Visa Acceptance](https://www.visaacceptance.com/en/solutions/payment-services/token-management-service.html)). Primer counts only payments that reached a processor, so fraud-screen blocks never enter the denominator ([Primer](https://primer.io/docs/observability/metrics)). Indian PSPs report end-to-end "success rate", and one glossary warns that first-attempt figures labelled "approval rate" cannot be compared with figures that count eventual success after retries ([PXP](https://www.pxp.io/payments-glossary/approval-rate)). Every uplift claim below has to be read against whichever of these the vendor uses. Merchants increasingly look past the raw auth rate anyway. In the MRC/Visa 2026 survey of **1,278 merchants in 37 countries**, "payment success rate" and revenue ranked as the two most important of 14 metrics, and authorization rate was secondary ([MRC/Visa 2026](https://www.visaacceptance.com/content/dam/documents/campaign/fraud-report/2026_Global_eCommerce_PaymentsandFraudReport.pdf)).

Errors in either direction cost money. Insufficient funds (code 51) is commonly cited at **about 44% of declines**, from an Ethoca study available only secondhand ([Chargeback Gurus](https://www.chargebackgurus.com/blog/credit-card-decline-codes)), and much of the remainder is the opaque "Do Not Honor" (05). False declines cost far more than fraud. Datos Insights models **$213bn of foregone revenue in 2025, rising to $297bn by 2029**, and an issuer-processing vendor estimates that a mid-sized issuer on legacy rules falsely declines about 30 good transactions for every fraud it stops ([The Fintech Times](https://thefintechtimes.com/bpc-banks-count-fraud-losses-but-not-what-false-declines-cost/)). Fraud is rising as well. In the 2026 MRC survey the average fraud rate by order rose from **3.0% to 3.5%**, and more merchants than a year earlier put their false-positive rate at 2–5%, which the report reads as rising "customer insults" ([MRC/Visa 2026](https://www.visaacceptance.com/content/dam/documents/campaign/fraud-report/2026_Global_eCommerce_PaymentsandFraudReport.pdf)). When both lines worsen at once, merchants need better decisioning, not simply tighter or looser rules. Estimates of how much more false declines cost than fraud (3x, 13x, 30x) do not hold up, but every source agrees that false declines cost more.

## Global PSPs advertise 1–4% average uplift on incomparable baselines

All four global PSPs now ship the same core stack: network tokens, account updater, ML-driven retries, issuer-specific message formatting, 3DS and exemption optimization, and acceptance analytics. Their headline claims look similar but measure different things.

| PSP | Headline claim (vendor-reported) | What is actually measured | Distinctive levers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stripe | **+3.8%** average acceptance from Authorization Boost; ~20% of false declines recovered; 1–2% uplift on "eligible volume" from Enhanced Issuer Network ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/authorization-boost)) | No baseline, sample or period disclosed; a localized Stripe page still cites **2.2%** for the same bundle ([Stripe MX](https://stripe.com/ja-mx/payments/authorization)) | Adaptive Acceptance, tokens on by default, real-time cross-brand account updater, Radar scores shared with issuers, Data Only 3DS and PINless debit retries (2026), standalone 3DS for other PSPs' volume |

| Braintree/PayPal | **+4.8 pp** from network tokens ([PayPal](https://www.paypal.com/us/brc/article/network-tokenization-and-lifecycle-management)); "4% average lift" ([PayPal](https://www.paypal.com/ca/enterprise/payment-processing/payment-optimization)) | Token data from Q4 2020–Q3 2021 in 8 markets; the lift graphic draws on an Oct 2022–Mar 2023 case | Smart Retries, ML Smart Routing, Direct Connect multi-processor redundancy, tokens for full-stack merchants |

The two "3.8" figures look like a tie but cannot be compared. Stripe states no baseline and does not say whether its figure is relative or absolute. Checkout.com states percentage points against a control group, runs 200+ live experiments at any time, and says about 70% of its payments now run on optimizations that began as experiments ([Checkout.com](https://www.checkout.com/blog/smart-ai-payment-optimization)). Adyen has moved its public KPI from authorization to conversion, which also captures payment-method ordering and shopper identification, so its numbers are the hardest to set beside the others. Braintree's evidence is the oldest, and PayPal's recent marketing emphasizes Fastlane guest-checkout conversion instead: around 80% for recognized shoppers on April–June 2024 data, which is a checkout metric rather than card approval ([PayPal](https://www.paypal.com/us/brc/article/one-click-guest-checkout-and-conversions)). The "up to" figures are best-case maxima, not expected results; examples include Checkout.com's "up to 9.5 percentage points" from its 2023 beta with 30+ merchants ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/checkoutcom-launches-intelligent-acceptance-an-ai-powered-optimization-engine-that-boosts-acceptance-rates-and-increases-merchant-revenue-301863558.html)) and Adyen's "up to 6%".

Beyond the shared stack, the four differ in four places. The first is owned acquiring. Adyen holds banking licences in the US, UK and EU, and Checkout.com went live with US direct acquiring under a Georgia MALPB charter on 9 September 2026 ([Checkout.com](https://www.checkout.com/newsroom/checkout-com-goes-live-with-us-direct-acquiring-under-malpb-charter)). The second is an issuer data loop. Stripe's Enhanced Issuer Network passes Radar scores to Capital One, Discover and Amex, and Stripe claims up to 8% fraud reduction alongside the 1–2% auth gain ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/newsroom/news/enhanced-issuer-network)). The third is retry guardrails, where Adyen candidly warns that its Excessive Retry Prevention Service "may have a negative impact on authorization rates" ([Adyen docs](https://docs.adyen.com/partners/uplift-enablement)). The fourth is US debit routing. Adyen's pilot with 20+ enterprise merchants delivered **26% cost savings with a 0.22% auth uplift** ([Adyen](https://www.adyen.com/press-and-media/adyens-intelligent-payment-routing-usdebit)), which shows that routing for cost and routing for approvals are separate objectives. Stripe is the most transparent on price. Its bundle costs 0.2% per successful transaction for custom-priced accounts; Adaptive Acceptance alone costs 0.08%, account updates $0.25 each, and token provisioning $0.15 on IC+ pricing ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/authorization-boost)).

Each PSP now bundles fraud tooling into its acceptance products, and the marketing has shifted from "fraud blocked" to "good customers wrongly blocked". Stripe Radar claims a 32% average fraud reduction, and one customer, Anthropic, cut wrongly blocked legitimate transactions by **83%** ([Stripe Radar](https://stripe.com/radar)). Adyen claims 42% fewer false positives. PayPal's March 2026 Fraud Protection Advanced study covered **only 36 merchants with no control group**; 58% of them cut issuer declines within three months and 76% of those also raised their auth rate, but the study gives no magnitudes ([PayPal](https://www.paypal.com/us/brc/article/auth-rate-increases-fraud-protection-advanced)). The PSPs are also automating the trade-off itself. Stripe is shipping automatic block-threshold adjustment, slated for general availability in Q3 2026 ([Stripe Sessions 2026](https://stripe.com/blog/everything-we-announced-at-sessions-2026)). Checkout.com requires every proposed optimization to report its "fraud implications" before a payments expert approves it ([Checkout.com](https://www.checkout.com/blog/smart-ai-payment-optimization)). Braintree makes its Chargeback Protection guarantee and its fraud tools mutually exclusive ([Braintree](https://developer.paypal.com/braintree/articles/guides/fraud-tools/premium/chargeback-protection)). None of the four publishes precision, recall or a portfolio-wide false-positive rate.

No independent dataset compares realized approval rates across these PSPs. The Forrester Wave for Merchant Payment Providers (Q1 2026) named Adyen, Stripe and Checkout.com Leaders, but it scores capabilities, not measured approvals ([Forrester](https://www.forrester.com/report/the-forrester-wave-tm-merchant-payment-providers-q1-2026/RES191722)). Even pricing teardowns conclude that "independent measurement on your own traffic is the only way to verify" ([PaymentBrief](https://paymentbrief.com/articles/stripe-vs-adyen-vs-checkout-2026-pricing-teardown/)). The PSPs now make that measurement possible. Stripe previewed A/B testing of Authorization Boost against a merchant's existing setup in April 2026 ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/blog/everything-we-announced-at-sessions-2026)), and Checkout.com shows control-group boost by attempt and by original decline code ([Checkout.com docs](https://www.checkout.com/docs/payments/optimize-payments/boost-acceptance-rates.md)). Merchants that already run sophisticated stacks should expect gains below the headline averages.

## Legacy acquirers trade on issuer ties; regional PSPs on local licences

Of the legacy acquirers, Worldpay publishes the most numbers; Global Payments closed its acquisition on 9 January 2026 at a **$24.25bn enterprise value** ([SEC 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1123360/000110465926002705/tm262856d1_8k.htm)). Worldpay reports a **5.5% increase in US authorization rates** from 3DS Flex, which chooses per transaction between full 3DS and data-only authentication. It also reports a **32% average reduction in false declines** from enhanced issuer data sharing, and both figures come from its own 2025 data ([Worldpay](https://worldpay.com/en/innovation-focus/keep-auth-rates-climbing)). Its Capital One Direct Data Share "could reduce false positive declines by 40%", a projection from a single issuer's internal results ([Worldpay](https://www.worldpay.com/en/press-releases/reducing-fraud-and-amping-authorizations-worldpay-and-capital)), and under its Auth Max Preferred program participating issuers relax decline rules for low-risk Worldpay transactions.

Cybersource, now Visa Acceptance Solutions, relies on Visa's network data. Its Token Management Service cites **+8% authorization for tokens versus PAN in European e-commerce**, from Visa Analytics Platform 2023 data ([Visa Acceptance](https://www.visaacceptance.com/en-ap/solutions/payment-services/token-management-service.html)). Its Decision Manager case studies quantify cuts in manual review (60% for an airline) and in fraud ratio (50% for Shopline), but give no approval or false-decline figures ([Cybersource](https://www.cas.cybersource.com/en/solutions/fraud-and-risk-management/decision-manager-plus-payer-authentication.html); [Visa Acceptance](https://www.visaacceptance.com/en-ap/case-studies/shopline.html)). Fiserv's Carat publishes mutually inconsistent figures for the same product ("up to 10%", "4–10%" and "7%"), none with a baseline, a pattern that points to marketing copy rather than a measured result ([Carat](https://www.carat.fiserv.com/content/dam/carat/us/en/pdf/optimization-checklist.pdf); [Carat DE](https://www.carat.fiserv.com/de-de/solutions/optimization/)). J.P. Morgan Payments, one of the largest US acquirers, publishes **no quantified uplift at all**. It describes data scientists deploying "six levers to increase authorization rates and three levers to reduce costs" ([J.P. Morgan](https://www.jpmorgan.com/content/dam/jpm/merchant-services/documents/simplifying-payments-for-your-business.pdf)).

The legacy acquirers' distinctive asset is their relationships with issuers. Programs such as Auth Max Preferred and the Capital One data share act on the issuer's decision itself. Once a merchant's own screening is tuned, that is the main lever left, and it requires acquiring scale; Stripe's Enhanced Issuer Network is the closest pure-play equivalent. The quantified false-decline claims rest on single-issuer or internal data, so they do not carry over to a different issuer mix.

Emerging-market PSPs make the largest claims, and they make them about acquiring geography rather than ML tuning.

| Provider | Published figure (vendor) | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| EBANX | International acquirers **30–50%** vs local **70–90%** approval ([EBANX](https://www.ebanx.com/en/insights/payments-explained/local-acquiring)); cross-border network tokens **+10 pp in Colombia, +7 pp in Peru**, fraud/security declines down "up to 86%" ([EBANX, Dec 2025](https://business.ebanx.com/en/press-room/press-releases/ebanx-drives-the-next-phase-of-credit-cards-in-latam-with-network-tokenization-for-cross-border-transactions)) | No method or date for the acquiring split; token figures stated in pp with merchant counts |
| Nuvei | Local acquiring "up to 16% higher acceptance"; cascading recovers one in four retried transactions ([Nuvei](https://nuvei.com/posts/the-2026-guide-to-global-payment-acceptance-local-acquiring-approval-rates-cross-border-optimization)); Mexico local 80%+ vs cross-border 50–60%, Caliente +10% after moving local ([Nuvei](https://www.nuvei.com/posts/evaluating-payment-strategies-for-the-mexican-market-direct-acquiring-versus-cross-border-models)) | No region, baseline or method |
| dLocal | No quantified approval figure; says domestic cards are "more likely to be accepted by local acquirers" ([dLocal](https://www.dlocal.com/press-releases/dlocal-and-paypal-expand-access-to-local-payments-across-emerging-markets)) | Qualitative only |
| Razorpay | UPI ~**99.2%** success vs cards 85–90%, international cards 70–80%; tokens up to +4.4%, cascading +14.8%, retries recover 15–20% of failures ([Razorpay](https://razorpay.com/blog/payment-success-rate-optimization-india/)) | End-to-end success rate, mostly unsourced; its cross-border figures are internally inconsistent |
| Juspay | No success-rate figure; offers reinforcement-learning gateway ordering, yet **95%+ of merchants use rule-based routing** ([Juspay](https://juspay.io/blog/juspay-orchestrator-and-merchant-controlled-routing-engine)) | Headline claim is cost, not approvals |
| Xendit | "Up to 30% increased acceptance rates" ([Xendit](https://docs.xendit.co/credit-cards)) | No methodology |

These magnitudes are vendor claims, but their direction matches the only network and regulator data available. Visa's 2016–17 data showed that in certain Latin American countries **2 in 10 e-commerce transactions were declined, versus 3 in 100 in store** ([Visa](https://caribbean.review.visa.com/partner-with-us/visa-performance-solutions/improving-the-credit-authorization-customer-experience.html)). The joint EBA–ECB report explains why issuers are cautious with foreign-acquired card-not-present payments: card fraud ran about **17 times higher when the payee sat outside the EEA**, where SCA is often not applied ([ECB](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/pr/date/2025/html/ecb.pr251215~e133d9d683.en.html)).

Issuer risk scoring is only one of several causes. Some domestic cards are not enabled for international use at all ([EBANX](https://www.ebanx.com/en/insights/payments-explained/local-acquiring)). Installment plans such as Mexico's *meses sin intereses*, which Nuvei says drive about 40% of Mexican e-commerce volume, get "limited or no support" through cross-border acquiring ([Nuvei](https://www.nuvei.com/posts/evaluating-payment-strategies-for-the-mexican-market-direct-acquiring-versus-cross-border-models)). Cross-border acquiring also adds international interchange, FX costs and slower settlement. Network tokens partly offset the "unknown foreign merchant" signal, which helps explain EBANX's 7–10 pp token gains.

Demand for the "global merchant, local processing" model is strong: dLocal's cross-border volume grew about 75% and exceeded half its total volume in Q2 2025 ([FXC Intelligence](https://www.fxcintel.com/research/reports/ct-dlocal-q2-2025-earnings)). Global acquirers mostly respond through partnerships rather than their own licences; Worldpay, for example, integrates EBANX for Latin American traffic ([EBANX](https://www.ebanx.com/en/ebanx-worldpay-integration/)). No credible approval data surfaced for Africa or MENA.

## Orchestration adds low single digits, and retry rules cap it

Orchestrators' average uplift claims cluster around 2–5 points, with case-study outliers at 6–11%, and no independent audit backs any of them. Primer advertises a **5% average uplift** and a 20% average recovery rate when a declined payment is immediately retried on an alternative processor ([Primer](https://primer.io/why-primer/boost-payment-success); [Primer Fallbacks](https://primer.io/optimize/fallbacks)). Its Ferryhopper case moved from **91.76% to 93.87%**, about +2.1 points, after multi-acquirer routing ([Orchestra Solutions](https://orchestrasolutions.com/how-a-multi-processor-strategy-can-improve-your-authorization-rates/)). Gr4vy's client Baby Bunting gained **2.8%** within four months of moving to dual acquirers with failover ([Gr4vy](https://gr4vy.com/posts/payment-orchestration-statistics-for-2026-market-size-adoption-and-merchant-results/)). Yuno claims about 8% average uplift from smart routing ([Yuno](https://y.uno/en/blog/the-merchants-with-the-highest-approval-rates-all-use-smart-routing)), and Payrails reports that inDrive approved 11% more transactions after multi-PSP routing, although inDrive also appears as a Yuno case study ([Payrails](https://www.payrails.com/blog/merchant-owned-payment-vault)). Aggregate estimates quoted by vendors run lower. One is a Capgemini figure of 2–3 points for dynamic routing ([Orchestra Solutions](https://orchestrasolutions.com/how-a-multi-processor-strategy-can-improve-your-authorization-rates/)); another is Solidgate's 2–4% for routing, local acquiring, tokens and adaptive fraud controls combined ([Solidgate](https://solidgate.com/blog/payment-orchestration-layer/)).

"Recovery rate" is not the same as auth-rate uplift. Suppose a merchant declines 10% of its traffic, only soft declines are eligible for a retry, and a fallback recovers 20% of those. The overall approval rate rises by only about 1–2 points. That arithmetic fits the most concrete case studies (+2.1 and +2.8 points), and it suggests the 5–11% claims combine tokens, local acquiring and retry logic, or start from a weak baseline.

PSP-native orchestration is still immature. **Stripe Orchestration remains in private preview**. It routes and retries card payments to third-party processors, but it does not handle disputes or settlement, and each processor remains processor of record with its own fees and liability ([Stripe docs](https://docs.stripe.com/payments/orchestration)). Stripe also treats a Radar fraud block as a decline that can be retried on the backup processor ([Stripe docs](https://docs.stripe.com/payments/orchestration/retries)). Merchants need to govern this deliberately, because cascading a fraud block defeats the fraud control. Adyen, by contrast, optimizes across its own rails through Uplift rather than routing to competitors. Adoption is mainstream: intelligent routing and SCA are the two most popular auth-improvement tactics, each used by about 4 in 10 merchants ([MRC/Visa 2026](https://www.visaacceptance.com/content/dam/documents/campaign/fraud-report/2026_Global_eCommerce_PaymentsandFraudReport.pdf)).

The trade-offs are concrete. When a PSP owns the token requestor ID, it owns the merchant's network tokens, and switching PSPs means re-collecting cards. A merchant-owned token requestor ID, held through a standalone token provider or an orchestrator, lets one token work across PSPs; without one, multi-PSP setups pay to tokenize the same card once per PSP ([Evervault](https://evervault.com/blog/evaluating-network-tokens-are-they-worth-it)). Splitting volume across PSPs also dilutes each PSP's ML training signal and weakens issuer-specific programs such as Stripe's Enhanced Issuer Network. That is an inference from how those features work, not a measured effect.

Network retry rules set a hard ceiling on any retry or cascade design. Since April 2022, Visa has charged for any reattempt on a "Category 1" never-retry code (04, 07, 12, 14, 15, 41, 43, 46, 57, R0, R1, R3). It also charges for each reattempt beyond **15 in 30 days** on retryable codes such as 51 and 91, at a fee cited as $0.10 domestic plus $0.05 cross-border, though other sources quote different amounts ([Payway](https://www.payway.com/visa-excessive-reattempts-rule-fees)). Mastercard's Transaction Processing Excellence program charges **$0.30–0.55 per excess attempt** beyond about 10 attempts in 24 hours or 35 in 30 days on the same card number. Brazil and Latin America use 7/35, and the US fee rose to $0.50 in January 2025 ([IXOPAY](https://www.ixopay.com/payment-knowledge-hub/mastercard-transaction-processing-excellence-excessive-authorizations-fee)). Mastercard's merchant advice codes say whether to retry at all: 03 means never retry, 21 means the recurring payment was cancelled, and codes 24–30 prescribe waits from 1 hour to 10 days ([Braintree](https://developer.paypal.com/braintree/docs/reference/general/merchant-responses/merchant-advice-codes/)). Secondary sources also report that since January 2026 Mastercard charges even on first-attempt CNP declines that return code 03 or 21, which Mastercard documentation does not confirm ([Merchant Cost Consulting](https://merchantcostconsulting.com/lower-credit-card-processing-fees/mastercard-transaction-processing-excellence-fees/)). The networks count attempts by card and merchant, not by PSP. A cascade to a second acquirer therefore still counts as a reattempt, and retrying hard declines through a different processor only adds fees.

## Visa's 1.5% VAMP line now binds the fraud side

The approval side of the trade-off is a revenue question. The fraud side is now increasingly governed by network monitoring programs, which have tightened sharply since 2025.

| Program | Metric | Merchant trigger | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Visa VAMP | (TC40 fraud reports + TC15 disputes) ÷ settled CNP transactions, **by count** | **≥150 bps** and ≥1,500 events/month in AP, Canada, EU, US (since 1 Apr 2026) and LAC; CEMEA ≥220 bps, ≥150 events, ≥$75k ([Visa](https://corporate.visa.com/content/dam/VCOM/corporate/visa-perspectives/security-and-trust/documents/visa-acquirer-monitoring-program-fact-sheet-2025.pdf)) | ~$8 per counted event, processor-reported ([Chargebacks911](https://chargebacks911.com/visa-acquirer-monitoring-program/)) |
| Visa VAMP (acquirer) | Same ratio across the portfolio | Above Standard ≥50 bps; Excessive ≥70 bps ([Visa](https://corporate.visa.com/content/dam/VCOM/corporate/visa-perspectives/security-and-trust/documents/visa-acquirer-monitoring-program-fact-sheet-2025.pdf)) | $4/$8 per event; remediation plans |
| Visa VAMP enumeration | Enumerated ÷ all authorizations | ≥20% and ≥300,000 enumerated | Monitoring; no fines per Stripe ([Stripe](https://docs.stripe.com/disputes/monitoring-programs)) |
| Visa Secure Excessive Fraud (US) | 3DS-authenticated fraud | ≥$75k and ≥0.9% | Loss of 3DS liability shift ([Stripe](https://docs.stripe.com/disputes/monitoring-programs)) |
| Mastercard ECM / HECM | Chargebacks ÷ prior-month transactions | 100–299 and 1.5–2.99% / ≥300 and ≥3% | Fines rising to $100k / $200k per month ([Stripe](https://docs.stripe.com/disputes/monitoring-programs)) |
| Mastercard EFM | Fraud chargebacks ÷ sales, **by value** | ≥$50k, ≥0.5%, ≥1,000 sales, **and** low 3DS share | Fines rising to $100k per month ([Checkout.com](https://www.checkout.com/docs/business-operations/manage-disputes/scheme-monitoring-programs.md)) |
| MATCH code 04 | Chargebacks ÷ sales | >1% and ≥$5k in one month, on termination | 5-year industry listing ([Stripe](https://docs.stripe.com/disputes/match)) |

VAMP changes the arithmetic in ways that loosen-the-rules strategies often miss, and Stripe's monitoring-program documentation spells out the mechanics ([Stripe](https://docs.stripe.com/disputes/monitoring-programs)). A fraudulent transaction that appears in both the TC40 fraud report and the TC15 dispute report "will be counted twice", so a merchant whose 0.75% chargeback rate is entirely third-party fraud is already near the 1.5% line. Fraud reports count regardless of outcome, and a refund does not remove either a fraud report or a dispute from the count. Issuers must report fraud on captured payments but not on uncaptured authorizations, so voiding a suspicious authorization before capture avoids the fraud report entirely. Vendors also report that fraud reports on 3DS-authenticated transactions still count ([Justt](https://justt.ai/vamp/)), which means a liability shift changes who pays for fraud, not whether it counts against the merchant. Visa excludes disputes resolved through pre-dispute tools, and fraud reports that qualify under Compelling Evidence 3.0, "contingent on the timing of the data extract" ([Visa](https://corporate.visa.com/content/dam/VCOM/corporate/visa-perspectives/security-and-trust/documents/visa-acquirer-monitoring-program-fact-sheet-2025.pdf)). Sources still disagree on whether fraud disputes resolved via RDR or CDRN drop out ([Ravelin](https://www.ravelin.com/blog/visa-vamp-changes-chargeback-disputes)).

Merchants below the threshold face pressure too, because acquirers are flagged at 50–70 bps across their whole portfolio. Stripe lists a merchant "Non-compliant" threshold of 0.5% and 5 events that applies when the acquirer is flagged, while Chargebacks911 cites fines for any merchant above 0.3%. Visa's new Merchant Elevated Risk Program in Asia Pacific went live on **9 October 2026**. It evaluates merchants early in their lifecycle, partly on authorization decline rate, against thresholds Visa does not publish ([Checkout.com](https://support.checkout.com/hc/en-us/articles/36650358354450-What-is-the-Visa-Merchant-Elevated-Risk-Program)).

Mastercard is moving toward Visa's model. One vendor's reading of the July 2026 bulletin says the Global Merchant Audit Program, starting 1 April 2027, combines confirmed fraud "including fraud that doesn't result in a chargeback" with non-fraud chargebacks. The same reading has the Excessive Chargeback Merchant (ECM) ratio phasing down to **0.9% by 2031** ([Chargebacks911](https://chargebacks911.com/global-merchant-audit-program-gmap/)); because it rests on a single vendor's interpretation, confirm it with an acquirer. Mastercard's Excessive Fraud Merchant program carries a 3DS carve-out: merchants whose 3DS share exceeds 10% of volume in non-regulated markets, or 50% in regulated ones, fall outside it. Sources disagree on which percentage applies where, but a modest 3DS share is still a cheap compliance lever for Mastercard e-commerce volume. Mastercard's Scam Merchant Monitoring Program also triggers an investigation when an approval rate drops by 50 points or more, or falls below 30%, over 72 hours ([Checkout.com](https://www.checkout.com/docs/business-operations/manage-disputes/scheme-monitoring-programs.md)), so an enumeration attack can become a compliance event.

The benchmarks show why these lines bind. Sift puts the average chargeback rate at **0.26% in Q3 2025, up 53% since Q1** ([Sift](https://sift.com/index-reports-disputes-q4-2025/)). Mastercard says **45% of 2024 chargebacks** were first- or third-party fraud ([The Fintech Times](https://thefintechtimes.com/chargebacks-have-a-bad-reputation-for-a-reason-mastercards-reveals-45-in-2024-were-fraudulent/)), and in 2026, 64% of merchants report rising first-party misuse ([MRC 2026](https://natlawreview.com/press-releases/merchant-risk-council-releases-2026-global-ecommerce-payments-fraud-report)).

No source states a recommended internal buffer, so the following target is an inference. MATCH listing becomes possible above 1%, acquirers are flagged at 0.5–0.7%, and PSPs pass that pressure down. The practical internal ceiling for most merchants is therefore a VAMP-equivalent ratio of roughly 0.3–0.5%, with escalation well before that. High-risk categories carry extra overhead on top. Crypto purchases (6051), skill gaming (5816) and gambling (7995) also need Visa Integrity Risk Program registration, reported at $950 per merchant ([Segpay](https://segpay.com/blog/understanding-visas-new-pricing-high-risk-tiers/)).

## Best practice widens the frontier with data before choosing a point on it

Leading practice distinguishes two kinds of moves. Tuning thresholds slides a merchant along a fixed trade-off curve: approve more and accept more fraud, or the reverse. Sending better data and smarter authentication shifts the curve itself, so approvals rise without extra fraud. The evidence favors doing the second before the first.

### Richer issuer data lifts approvals and fraud detection together

Visa's Better Data guide uses Feb–Mar 2022 data covering 95%+ of Visa Secure transactions. Merchants completing the 3DS Method URL at least 95% of the time saw **+8% approval and a 127% frictionless-rate lift** compared with lower-completion merchants. Merchants that populated more than half of the priority data elements, such as browser IP, device data, billing and shipping address, email and phone, saw **+6% approval**. With priority data present, issuers gained up to a **65% lift in fraud detection** ([Visa](https://corporate.visa.com/content/dam/VCOM/corporate/solutions/documents/global-better-data-best-practices-guide-for-visa-secure-with-emv.pdf)). This is a cross-sectional comparison, not a controlled test.

The controlled evidence comes from Visa and Adyen. In a pilot with an enterprise travel merchant from **8 October 2025 to 22 January 2026**, they fed enhanced data through Visa IDX (formerly Data Only) into Visa's issuer risk scoring with no added consumer friction. Against a control group of standard unauthenticated transactions, first-attempt approvals rose **+290 bps cross-border, +190 bps US domestic, +310 bps on PAN and +170 bps on network tokens, with fraud rates stable**. The lift was largest on transactions that had previously been declined as suspected fraud. The pilot covered only a subset of optimized US issuers and excluded wallets, standard 3DS and merchant-initiated transactions ([Visa–Adyen](https://www.visa.com/vcom-assets/content/dam/visa/reimagine-visa/business/programs/documents/visa.-adyen-case-study.pdf)). Visa has since formalized the approach in its Digital Commerce Authentication Program, built around four fields: device ID, billing address, IP and email ([Visa DCAP](https://www.visa.com/en-us/business/programs/dcap)).

Network tokens work the same way, but the gain concentrates in card-on-file traffic. Visa's footnoted figure is **4.6% higher CNP approvals**, from Oct–Dec 2022 first-attempt data ([Visa Acceptance](https://www.visaacceptance.com/en/solutions/payment-services/token-management-service.html)). In September 2025 Visa's chief product officer said tokenized transactions are about 5 points more likely to complete and carry a **40% lower fraud rate**, while Mastercard's CFO cited 3–6 points ([Payments Dive](https://www.paymentsdive.com/news/visa-mastercard-push-more-tokenization/759762)). A Mastercard white paper co-authored with Checkout.com (April 2026) reports +10.3 points on first attempts and 49% fewer fraud chargebacks. That figure comes from one acquirer's FY2025 merchant base, where selection effects likely inflate it ([TechAfrica News](https://techafricanews.com/?p=77973)). At the other end, Evervault estimates that merchants with few repeat customers see only **30–50 bps** from tokens ([Evervault](https://evervault.com/blog/evaluating-network-tokens-webinar-q-and-a)).

Issuer models also learn a merchant's fraud profile over rolling 30/60/90-day windows, and PayPal attributes post-deployment drops in issuer declines to exactly this effect ([PayPal](https://www.paypal.com/us/brc/article/auth-rate-increases-fraud-protection-advanced)). Cutting fraud therefore raises approvals with a lag, and short pre/post tests understate the gain. The sources imply a sensible sequence. A merchant first completes the 3DS Method URL and sends full priority data. It then adds data-only authentication on non-SCA flows and network tokens with cryptograms for stored cards. Finally, it sets merchant-initiated and stored-credential flags correctly and retries in line with the merchant advice codes.

### 3DS is an exemption game in Europe and a scalpel in the US

In the EEA, SCA is mandatory and it works. The EBA–ECB found SCA-authenticated transactions less likely to be fraudulent, while fraudsters shifted toward exempted payments and social engineering ([EBA](https://www.eba.europa.eu/publications-and-media/press-releases/joint-eba-ecb-report-payment-fraud-strong-authentication-remains-effective-fraudsters-are-adapting)). In Europe, the optimization happens in the exemptions. The transaction-risk-analysis (TRA) exemption allows remote card payments without SCA up to set amounts, provided the PSP's fraud rate stays under a ceiling for each band.

| Transaction amount | Maximum PSP fraud rate |
|---|---|
| Up to €100 | 0.13% |
| Up to €250 | 0.06% |
| Up to €500 | 0.01% |

The bands come from the EBA's regulatory technical standards ([EBA RTS Annex](https://eba.europa.eu/sites/default/files/documents/10180/1894900/2b6be09b-f3db-40e4-b0ee-906bf65e7c82/RTS%20on%20SCA%20and%20CSC%20-%20Annex%20Table%2028%20-%20June%202017.pdf)). The fraud rate that counts belongs to the acquirer or issuer, not the merchant, so routing to low-fraud acquirers expands a merchant's exemption headroom ([Fraud Practice](https://www.fraudpractice.com/post/processing-through-acquirers-with-lower-fraud-rates-reduces-the-need-for-sca-under-psd2)).

That headroom matters more each year. Ravelin's 2026 report shows frictionless rates flat or falling in **28 of 37 countries**, with 78% of merchants now using exemptions. 3DS success varies widely by country, from **95% in the UK and 88% in the US to 36% in Brazil** ([Ravelin](https://www.ravelin.com/blog/3d-secure-rates-2026.md)). Ravelin advises choosing which exemption to request issuer by issuer rather than always asking for the highest one. Checkout.com notes that some French banks change their exemption preference with ticket size ([Checkout.com](https://www.checkout.com/blog/smart-ai-payment-optimization)).

The US behaves differently. In Stripe's 2024 test, a group of businesses averaged 87% approval before adding 3DS. Challenged-and-authenticated transactions stayed at 87%, but **frictionless-path transactions fell to 82%**. In the US, authorization and authentication rates "tended to vary inversely", while in the EU and UK they rose together ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/blog/surprising-findings-from-our-analysis-of-3ds-transactions-in-the-us)). US best practice is therefore no 3DS by default. Merchants invoke it selectively for the risk-score grey band, for fraud-prone high-ticket orders, and to raise 3DS coverage enough to stay out of Mastercard's Excessive Fraud Merchant program. Data-only flows, such as Stripe's and Worldpay's 3DS Flex, give issuers the data without the friction.

Liability shift is not free insurance. Authenticated fraud still counts in VAMP, and Visa's US program strips the shift entirely at $75k and 0.9%. Europe's PSD3/PSR reforms will not change any of this soon. Political agreement came on 27 November 2025, and most obligations are not expected to apply before late 2027 or 2028 ([Ideem](https://www.useideem.com/post/psr3-psd3-implementation-update-mid-2026)).

### Block thresholds should follow margin, with step-up for the grey band

The standard framework is expected-value thresholding on a risk score, and Stripe's primer works through an example. An average $26 sale at an 8% margin earns $2.08. A fraudulent sale costs $23.92 in goods plus a $15 chargeback fee, or $38.92, so one fraud wipes out the profit of about 18.7 good sales. Blocking therefore pays only if **more than about 5% of the blocked set is actually fraud** ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/en-gb-us/radar/guide)). Because break-even precision follows from margin, high-margin merchants should tolerate more fraud than low-margin ones. Single-order economics also understates the cost of a false decline: in a survey Stripe cites, 33% of consumers said they would not return after one ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/en-gb-us/radar/guide)). Customer lifetime value belongs in the false-positive cost.

Network constraints turn the problem into a constrained optimization. Near a VAMP threshold, each fraud event adds roughly $8 in fees plus termination risk, which pushes the threshold toward catching more fraud. VAMP counts events while Mastercard's fraud program measures value. Low-ticket digital goods are therefore more exposed to Visa's ratio, and high-ticket electronics or travel to Mastercard's, so thresholds should be segmented by ticket size. Both points are inferences from how the programs are built.

Between "approve" and "block" sits a step-up band, and leading merchants price friction like any other decision. Stripe notes that routing some classes of transaction to manual review "gain[s] precision without a hit to recall", at the cost of human work ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/en-gb-us/radar/guide)). Airbnb set its model thresholds by minimizing a loss function across false positives, false negatives and true positives, and it applied ML-triggered friction rather than blanket blocks ([InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2018/03/financial-fraud-ml-airbnb/)). Uber uses penny-drop verification to confirm card ownership, and it trains models on TC40 fraud reports, which arrive before chargebacks ([Uber Engineering](https://www.uber.com/blog/how-uber-processes-early-chargeback-signals)).

Card-testing defense now has a hard compliance target in Visa's 20% enumeration ratio. J.P. Morgan advises merchants not to rely on chargebacks to detect card testing, because the windows are too long, and to monitor authorization-decline patterns instead ([J.P. Morgan](https://JPMorgan.com/content/dam/jpm/securities/documents/card-testing-white-paper-ada-final.pdf)). Stripe pairs rate limiters, ML models and CAPTCHA triggers ([Stripe](https://docs.stripe.com/card-testing)). Before shipping a new fraud model, Stripe checks it against false-positive rate, block rate and authorization rate, overall and per merchant, and treats a model whose higher recall spikes block rates as a failure ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/en-gb-us/radar/guide)).

### Pre-dispute deflection, not representment, protects the ratio

Monitoring programs "don't consider dispute outcomes", and they care more about how well a merchant prevents disputes than whether it wins them ([Stripe](https://docs.stripe.com/disputes/monitoring-programs)). Merchants win about 43.8% of representments, but net recovery is only **10.7% of chargebacks** ([Chargebacks911](https://chargebacks911.com/chargeback-stats/)). Representment recovers money; it does not fix a ratio.

The sources imply a ranking of tools by how much they protect the ratio. The strongest is voiding a suspicious payment before capture, because no fraud report then has to be filed. Next comes Compelling Evidence 3.0 used pre-dispute through Order Insight. It blocks the dispute, leaves liability with the issuer, and "does not affect fraud or chargeback ratios". It requires two prior undisputed transactions on the same card, 120–365 days earlier, matching on at least two of four data elements, one of which must be IP address or device ID ([Chargebacks911](https://chargebacks911.com/prevent-chargebacks/prevent-visa-disputes/visa-compelling-evidence-3-0/)). Third are RDR, CDRN and Ethoca refunds, which remove the dispute, at least for non-fraud cases, but may leave a fraud report. Representment comes last: it recovers money but leaves the ratio unchanged.

Alerts cost roughly **$15 per RDR or CDRN case and about $29 per Ethoca alert** through resellers ([Chargeback.io](https://www.chargeback.io/blog/rdr-vs-cdrn-vs-ethoca)). They pay off mainly for merchants near a threshold or with high ticket sizes, and merchants should avoid enrolling the same transactions in two alert networks and paying twice ([Aurai](https://aurai.dev/blog/ethoca-verifi-alerts-economics)). With first-party misuse rising, these tools have become ratio-management infrastructure rather than back-office cost control.

### One P&L, holdouts and matured disputes keep the balance honest

Leading merchants put approvals and fraud under one objective. The metric is net approved good revenue minus total fraud cost, where fraud cost covers losses, fees, review, vendor costs and lost margin on false declines. They pair it with a written metric dictionary, because payments teams tend to measure eventual success while finance measures first-attempt approvals ([Primer](https://primer.io/blog/payment-metrics-and-kpis)). They also guard explicitly against fraud tools that cut chargebacks while silently raising false declines ([IXOPAY](https://www.ixopay.com/payment-knowledge-hub/successful-authorization-rate-kpi)).

Any claim of "+X points approvals, −Y% fraud", whether from a PSP or a fraud vendor, should pass a properly designed test. Randomization belongs at the card or customer level, not the transaction level, so that retries do not contaminate the groups. A small holdout of would-be-blocked traffic estimates the false-positive rate. Results should be read on first-attempt, issuer-level metrics, stratified by issuer, BIN country and ticket size. The verdict should wait until dispute windows mature, with early fraud reports giving a preliminary read in the meantime. The Visa–Adyen pilot is the public template, with its control group, first-attempt measurement and explicit exclusions. Guarantee vendors such as Signifyd, Riskified and Forter, which cover fraud chargebacks on the orders they approve ([Ringly](https://www.ringly.io/blog/riskified-alternatives)), have an incentive to decline more. Their approval rates should be compared on matched traffic, not taken from case studies.

## Conclusion

The evidence reverses how auth rate is usually bought. Merchants shop for the PSP with the best ML engine. Yet the differences among top-tier global engines are worth perhaps 1–4 points that nobody has independently verified, while structural choices move tens of points (local acquiring in emerging markets) or hundreds of basis points with no added fraud (issuer data and tokens). The practical implication is to run procurement as an experiment. Use the A/B and control-group tooling Stripe and Checkout.com now expose, insist on first-attempt, issuer-level definitions, and own the token requestor ID before going multi-PSP. Otherwise orchestration's modest gains get paid for in lost token portability and diluted issuer signal.

The fraud side is tightening in a specific direction. Both networks are converging on counting fraud whether or not a chargeback follows: Visa's fraud reports already count toward a 1.5% VAMP line, Mastercard's audit program follows from 2027, and its ECM ratio is heading toward 0.9%. Neither liability shift nor dispute wins cleans the ratio any more, which ends the old playbook of loosening rules and relying on 3DS liability shift and representment. The trade-off is less zero-sum than it looks, but only for merchants that do the data work first. Merchants that do not will find the tightening network lines forcing them down the curve toward lower approvals.
