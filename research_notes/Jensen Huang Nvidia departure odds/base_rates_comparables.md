# Base Rates and Comparables for Departure of a Long-Tenured Founder-CEO (age 63, 33 years in role, mega-cap tech), plus Base Rates for CEOs Under Personal-Conduct Board Investigations

Research date: November 12, 2026. Scope: base rates and comparable cases only. Nvidia- and Huang-specific facts (stake size, board makeup, details of the November 2026 allegations) are covered by other researchers and are not verified here. Per the coordinator's update, Huang is in a live scandal: an admitted affair (Nov 8, 2026) with an alleged Chinese intelligence asset tied to Nvidia customer Megaspeed, a second reported affair (Nov 11) with a subordinate VP, a board investigation underway, and a WSJ report that the board is preparing to oust him. Section 5 covers the conditional base rate for that situation. Sections 1 to 4 are the unconditional (no-scandal) baseline.

## 1. Annual CEO turnover rates for S&P 500 and large-cap US companies (2024 to 2026), differences by founder status, tenure and age, and average retirement age

### Takeaway
S&P 500 CEO turnover runs about 10 to 13% a year: 10% in 2024 and 12 to 13% in 2025, which was a multi-year high. 2026 is running lower. Departing S&P 500 CEOs averaged 62.9 years old and about 8.4 to 9 years in tenure (11.3 years in tech), so a 63-year-old is at the typical departure age, while 33 years of tenure is an extreme outlier. CEO exits spike at age 65. No industry report breaks out turnover for founders or for CEOs with 20 or more years in the job. Founders are less likely to be forced out (see section 4).

### Cited Findings
**Overall turnover levels**
- Spencer Stuart counted 59 new S&P 500 CEOs in 2025, up 11% from 53 in 2024, and 168 new S&P 1500 CEOs, the most since 2010. 59 out of 500 is about 11.8% turnover. Technology, media and telecom had 33 transitions, nearly double 2024's 18, for 14% annual turnover in that sector (236 active CEOs). — [Spencer Stuart, 2025 S&P 1500 CEO Transitions (Feb 2026)](https://www.spencerstuart.com/research-and-insight/2025-sp-1500-ceo-transitions-behind-the-ceo-moment); [PDF](https://www.spencerstuart.com/-/media/2026/02/ceo-transitions-us/2025-s-p-1500-ceo-transitions-behind-the-ceo-moment.pdf)
- The Conference Board projected a 13% S&P 500 succession rate for 2025 (as of October), up from 10% in 2024. The Russell 3000 rate was 11%. — [finanzen.net summary of the Conference Board report](https://www.finanzen.net/nachricht/aktien/report-ceo-departures-are-rising-even-at-strong-performing-companies-15309219); [Conference Board, CEO Succession Practices 2025 Edition](https://www.conference-board.org/publications/CEO-succession-practices-2025-edition)
- The historical S&P 500 succession rate has averaged about 10.9% (the 2018 rate was 11.8%). — [Conference Board via Harvard Law School Forum, 2019](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2019/12/16/ceo-succession-practices-2019-edition)
- 2026 is running slower. Russell Reynolds counted 101 global CEO departures in H1 2026, a nine-year low, down from 118 in H1 2025. 2025 had 234 departures, an eight-year high. — [Gulf News on Russell Reynolds](https://gulfnews.com/business/corporate-news/global-ceo-exits-slump-to-nine-year-low-insiders-preferred-1.500703302); [Harvard Forum, Global CEO Turnover Index (Aug 2026)](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2026/08/13/global-ceo-turnover-index/)
- Challenger, Gray & Christmas counted 1,040 US CEO exits through July 2026, down 23% from the same period of 2025. This covers all US organizations, not just large caps. — [Challenger July 2026 report](https://www.challengergray.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/July-26-CEO-Turnover-Report.pdf); [Challenger CEO turnover blog](https://www.challengergray.com/blog/tag/ceo-turnover/)

**Forced exits**
- Roughly 1 in 7 CEO successions were forced in both 2024 and 2025. S&P 500 forced departures rose from 7 in 2024 to 10 in 2025. In the Russell 3000, underperformance was the most common reason (44% of forced exits in 2025). In the S&P 500, activists drove 8 of 19 forced departures from 2024 through August 2026. — [Conference Board, "Forced CEO Departures," Harvard Forum, Sept 19, 2026](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2026/09/19/forced-ceo-departures/)

**Tenure and age at departure**
- Departing S&P 1500 CEOs averaged 8.5 years of tenure in 2025, down from 9.2 in 2024 and a 2021 peak of 10.3. Departing tech CEOs averaged the longest tenure of any sector, 11.3 years. 45% of S&P 500 CEOs who left had served five years or less. — [Spencer Stuart PDF](https://www.spencerstuart.com/-/media/2026/02/ceo-transitions-us/2025-s-p-1500-ceo-transitions-behind-the-ceo-moment.pdf)
- The Conference Board puts average tenure of departing S&P 500 CEOs at 9 years in 2025, up from 7 in 2024. This conflicts somewhat with Spencer Stuart's figure; the two use different datasets. — [finanzen.net](https://www.finanzen.net/nachricht/aktien/report-ceo-departures-are-rising-even-at-strong-performing-companies-15309219)
- Average age of departing CEOs in 2025 was 61.8 for the S&P 1500 and 62.9 for the S&P 500, where it has been rising for several years. In financial services, 18% of departing CEOs were in their 70s. — [Spencer Stuart PDF](https://www.spencerstuart.com/-/media/2026/02/ceo-transitions-us/2025-s-p-1500-ceo-transitions-behind-the-ceo-moment.pdf)
- In 2024 the Conference Board found that succession rates for S&P 500 CEOs aged 64 and older fell. It warned of a possible "retirement cliff" as CEOs stay longer. — [Harvard Forum, Nov 2024](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2024/11/19/ceo-succession-practices-in-the-russell-3000-and-sp-500-3)
- In a sample of 5,841 ExecuComp CEOs (32,026 CEO-years, 1992 to 2008), CEO turnover spikes at age 65 and is consistently higher after 65 than before. A 1987 study (Vancil) found 47% of 421 firms had mandatory CEO retirement at 65. US law bars mandatory retirement of executives before 65. — [Jenter & Lewellen, NBER w17663](https://www.nber.org/papers/w17663.pdf)

**Executive-chair transitions**
- In 2025, 23% of S&P 500 CEO transitions kept the outgoing CEO on as executive chair, 3% named a different executive chair, and 74% had no executive chair. Across the S&P 1500, 17% kept the outgoing CEO as executive chair, down from 30% two years earlier. — [Spencer Stuart PDF](https://www.spencerstuart.com/-/media/2026/02/ceo-transitions-us/2025-s-p-1500-ceo-transitions-behind-the-ceo-moment.pdf)

### Inferences
- Generic S&P 500 baseline: a constant 10 to 12% annual hazard gives about 10 to 12% over 1 year, 27 to 32% over 3 years, and 41 to 47% over 5 years. The 3- and 5-year figures are my arithmetic.
- Several things push a long-tenured founder's hazard below that baseline. About 1 in 7 exits are forced for underperformance or activism, and a successful founder faces little of that. Most short-tenure turnover (45% of S&P 500 exits come within 5 years) does not apply after 33 years. One factor pushes the other way: at 63 to 68 he moves through the age-65 retirement spike.
- Unconditional (pre-scandal) estimate for a founder-CEO of this profile, built from sections 1 to 4: about 7% (range 4 to 12%) within 1 year, about 22% (15 to 35%) within 3 years, and about 38% (28 to 55%) within 5 years. This assumes hazards of roughly 7% a year in years 1 and 2, rising to roughly 11 to 12% by year 5. The most likely form of exit would be a planned move to executive chair (see section 3).
- Planned mega-cap transitions are announced months ahead (see section 3). With no announcement, the near-term part of the unconditional 1-year probability comes mostly from unplanned events: health, death, or scandal. The scandal is now that event; see section 5.

### Gaps
- No public dataset gives annual turnover for founder-CEOs, CEOs with 20 or more (or 30 or more) years of tenure, or by single year of age for 2023 to 2026. Jenter & Lewellen's age-specific turnover rates appear only in a chart, so exact values are not available as text.
- The 2025 Conference Board breakdown by age (64 and older) and by reason (retirement, health, death) was behind a paywall or truncated.
- An unsourced search snippet said more than 11% of S&P 500 CEOs are aged 65 to 69. I could not attribute it to a specific source, so it is excluded.

## 2. Actuarial risk: mortality for a US male aged 63 to 68, cumulative 1/3/5-year mortality, socioeconomic adjustment, and health-forced exits

### Takeaway
For an average US male at 63 to 64, the chance of dying is about 1.6% within 1 year, 5% within 3 years, and 9% within 5 years. For a very wealthy man these figures are plausibly about half: roughly 0.8%, 2.6%, and 4.6%. Death or incapacity is a small part of the unconditional departure probability and a negligible part next to the scandal channel.

### Cited Findings
- CDC NVSR Vol. 74 No. 2 (April 2025), US life table for males, 2022. Probability of dying within the year (qx) by age: 62–63: 0.014542; 63–64: 0.015518; 64–65: 0.016545; 65–66: 0.017652; 66–67: 0.018999; 67–68: 0.020295; 68–69: 0.021722; 69–70: 0.023220; 70–71: 0.024718. Life expectancy at 63 is 18.9 years. — [CDC NVSR 74-2 Table 2 (copy at lifetable.de)](https://www.lifetable.de/File/GetDocument/data\USA\USA000020222022CU1.pdf); [spreadsheet](https://ftp.cdc.gov/pub/Health_Statistics/NCHS/Publications/NVSR/74-02/Table02.xlsx)
- The SSA 2022 period life table (2025 Trustees Report) gives similar male figures: 0.015571 at exact age 63, 0.016737 at 64, 0.017897 at 65, and about 0.019017 at 66. Life expectancy at 63 is 18.89 years. These come from search snippets; ssa.gov blocked direct fetching. — [SSA Actuarial Life Table](https://www.ssa.gov/oact/STATS/table4c6.html); [SSA table, 2025 TR](https://www.ssa.gov/oact/STATS/table4c6_2022_TR2025.html)
- Income and longevity: a 40-year-old man in the top 1% of income has a life expectancy of 87.3 years, against 72.7 for the bottom 1%, a 14.6-year gap. Between 2001 and 2014, top-5% men gained 2.34 years. — [Fortune on Chetty et al., JAMA 2016](https://fortune.com/2016/04/11/one-percent-health-decade/); [CNN Money](https://money.cnn.com/2016/04/11/news/economy/life-expectancy-rich-poor/)
- CEO-specific mortality: in a study of more than 1,600 large-company US CEOs, industry downturns during a CEO's tenure cut lifespan by about 1.5 years, and anti-takeover protection added about 2 years. Each extra year of age raised CEO mortality hazard by about 12%. — [Borgschulte, Guenzel, Liu & Malmendier, "CEO Stress, Aging, and Death"](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~ulrike/Papers/CEO_Stress.pdf); [Harvard Forum summary](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2021/04/29/ceo-stress-aging-and-death)
- Health precedent: Steve Jobs, then 56, had been on medical leave since January 2011. He resigned as Apple CEO on August 24, 2011, stayed on as chairman, and died October 5, 2011. — [MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2018/08/24/this-day-in-2011-steve-jobs-resigns-as-ceo/); [Cult of Mac](https://www.cultofmac.com/apple-history/steve-jobs-resigns-apple-ceo)

### Inferences
- Cumulative male mortality computed from the CDC qx values, starting at age 63.75 (my calculation):
  - Population average: 1.6% (1 year), 5.1% (3 years), 9.0% (5 years).
  - Hazard ×0.75: 1.2%, 3.9%, 6.8%.
  - Hazard ×0.5, a plausible top-1% adjustment consistent with the Chetty gap: 0.8%, 2.6%, 4.6%.
- Serious illness or incapacity forcing an exit plausibly adds a comparable or somewhat smaller amount. Overall, health and death likely contribute about 1 to 2% a year to the departure hazard.

### Gaps
- I found no clean statistic on the share of large-cap CEO successions caused by death or illness. Spencer Stuart and Conference Board track it, but the figures were not accessible. Nor did I find a rate of disabling health events among executives in their 60s.
- The ×0.5 SES multiplier is an informed assumption, not a figure taken from a source.

## 4. Academic research on founder-CEO tenure and departure hazard

### Takeaway
There is no published large-cap hazard model by founder status, age and tenure that gives clean annual probabilities. The evidence shows a sharp age-65 turnover spike, founders being less likely to be fired for poor performance, and founder exits being more often voluntary.

### Cited Findings
- CEO turnover spikes at 65 and stays higher afterward. Earlier studies found the same: Gibbons & Murphy 1992, Murphy & Zimmerman 1993, Weisbach 1995. Retirement-age CEOs are also 50% more likely to receive takeover bids (about 6% vs 4% a year). — [Jenter & Lewellen, NBER w17663](https://www.nber.org/papers/w17663.pdf)
- Jenter & Lewellen's work on performance-induced turnover finds that even retirement-age CEO exits are more likely after poor performance, so "voluntary" retirements are partly tied to performance. — [search summary of Jenter & Lewellen LSE paper](https://personal.lse.ac.uk/jenter/CEO%20turnover%202020-03-10.pdf)
- In a study of 484 CEO turnovers from 2000 to 2015, a poorly performing founder-CEO was less likely to be fired than an outsider CEO. — [Kowalewski working paper (IESEG)](https://www.ieseg.fr/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/2019-ACF-01_Kowalewski.pdf)
- After accounting irregularities at newly public firms, non-founder CEOs turned over at 49% versus 29% for founder CEOs. Founder exits are more often voluntary than involuntary. Lee, Yoon & Boivie (AMJ 2020) found that 123 of 448 founder-CEO IPO firms went through a succession event. — [Family Business summary](https://www.familybusiness.org/content/what-makes-founders-leave-their-companies)
- Founder-CEO firms earned benchmark-adjusted excess returns of 8.3% a year from 1993 to 2002 (4.4% with controls). — [Fahlenbrach, JFQA 2009](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:cup:jfinqa:v:44:y:2009:i:02:p:439-466_09)
- Strategy& found that successors to long-serving "legend" CEOs struggle. — [PwC press release 2019](https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/news-room/press-releases/2019/ceo-turnover-record-high.html); [strategy+business](https://strategy-business.com/article/Succeeding-the-long-serving-legend-in-the-corner-office)

### Inferences
- Founder status mainly lowers the forced, performance-driven part of the hazard. It does less to lower the retirement part. Section 5 shows it gives only partial protection against conduct scandals.

### Gaps
- I found no study that publishes annual departure probabilities for founder-CEOs aged 60 to 70 or with more than 20 years of tenure.

## 5. Base rates for CEOs facing board investigations over personal conduct or undisclosed relationships (added per coordinator update)

### Takeaway
In high-profile large-company cases where a board investigation confirmed an undisclosed relationship, a relationship with a subordinate, or a related conflict of interest, hired CEOs almost always left. In 9 of the 9 cases below the CEO exited, usually within days to about 6 weeks of board involvement or the press report. The longest took about 3 to 5 months, with a second probe (Nestlé). Two things were consistently decisive: a second wave of allegations, and evidence the CEO had not been candid with the board (Looney, Freixe, Hurd). Founders fare only somewhat better. Wynn, Kalanick and Neumann still left within days to weeks once boards or investors turned. The survivors are founders with control or an overwhelming employee and investor revolt (Altman), or those whose boards took no formal action (Musk). The broader academic sample, which includes milder incidents, shows most accused executives keep their jobs, so the high-profile list overstates the exit rate for minor incidents.

### Cited Findings
**Aggregate data**
- Among the world's 2,500 largest companies in 2018, ethical lapses caused 39% of the 89 forced CEO exits, more than financial performance (35%) or board conflict (13%), for the first time in the study's history. In 2008 the share was 10%. The category includes fraud, bribery and sexual indiscretions. — [Consulting.us on Strategy& study](https://www.consulting.us/news/2248/more-ceos-ousted-for-ethical-lapses-finds-pwc-strategy-study); [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2019/05/15/pwc-strategy-report-top-reason-ceos-were-ousted-in-2018-was-scandals.html); [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2019/05/20/725108825/top-reason-for-ceo-departures-among-largest-companies-is-now-misconduct-study-fi)
- Academic study of executive personal indiscretions: most accused executives keep their jobs. A conference version reports about 25% turnover for first offenders and 27% for repeat offenders. Firms lose value, about 4.1% (around $226M) when the CEO is involved, and dishonesty does the most damage. Indiscretions are more common at poorly governed firms, where disciplinary turnover is less likely. — [Cline, Walkling & Yore, JFE 2018](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:127:y:2018:i:2:p:389-415); [AEA conference version](https://topcat.aeaweb.org/conference/2014/retrieve.php?pdfid=797); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/759278)

**Hired (non-founder) CEOs**
- **Mark Hurd (HP, 2010):** Harassment complaint by a former contractor in late June 2010. Resigned August 6, 2010, about 5 to 6 weeks later. No harassment was found, but he had failed to disclose a personal relationship with the contractor and filed inaccurate expense reports. — [Palo Alto Online](https://www.paloaltoonline.com/news/2010/08/06/woman-identified-in-hp-ceo-mark-hurd-resignation); [eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/pc-hardware/hp-s-hurd-resignation-brought-questions-about-scandal-company-future/)
- **Darren Huston (Priceline, 2016):** Whistleblower tip led to an investigation run by independent directors and outside counsel. He resigned April 28, 2016, with no severance and reportedly forfeited more than $13M in equity. The employee was not in his reporting line. — [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2016/04/28/priceline-ceo-darren-huston-steps-down.html); [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/priceline-ceo-resigns-after-probe-into-employee-relationship)

### Inferences
- **Hired CEOs.** All 9 high-profile cases where an investigation confirmed a relationship or conflict policy breach ended in exit. Median time from board engagement or press report to exit was about 1 to 6 weeks. Only Freixe, with two probes, took months. Boards often say the exit is unrelated to performance (McDonald's, Priceline, Kohl's), so strong performance did not protect Easterbrook or Huston.
- **Founders.** Of the founders under serious board or investor pressure, Wynn, Kalanick and Neumann left within days to weeks. Altman was ousted and then reinstated within about 5 days. Musk faced no formal action. Founder protection depends mainly on voting control or on an overwhelming employee and investor revolt, not on founder status itself.
- **Features of the reported Huang situation that match the strongest exit predictors** (relying on the coordinator's description, not verified here):
  - A relationship with a subordinate, a standard policy breach (Krzanich, Easterbrook, Freixe).
  - A partner tied to a counterparty, a conflict of interest (Buchanan's vendor, Hurd's contractor).
  - Allegations arriving in sequence (Looney, Freixe, Moonves, where the second wave was decisive).
  - A reported board move to oust (Moonves, Wynn, Kalanick all left within days to weeks of such reports).
  - An added national-security and export-control angle that has no close precedent. It likely raises board, regulator and customer pressure.
- **Conditional estimate** (judgment, not a sourced statistic): given an active board investigation and credible reports that the board is preparing to oust him, the precedents point to about 65 to 85% probability of leaving the CEO role within about 3 months, and about 75 to 90% within 1 year. The main path to survival is an Altman-style rally of employees and investors, or an investigation that substantially clears the conduct and candor questions. Looney is the precedent for surviving a first round; he still fell to a second.
- **Form of exit.** In misconduct cases the CEO usually leaves the company entirely or resigns "effective immediately," often with no severance and with equity forfeited (Huston, Buchanan, Freixe). Founders sometimes kept board seats or a non-executive chair (Kalanick, Neumann). None kept an executive-chair role.
- **Overall 1/3/5-year odds.** Combining the conditional scandal probability with the unconditional baseline from sections 1 to 4, departure probabilities are dominated by the scandal channel: roughly 75 to 90% (1 year), 80 to 92% (3 years), 85 to 95% (5 years). If the investigation clears him, they revert toward the section 1 baseline of about 7%, 22% and 38%.

### Gaps
- No systematic dataset links board investigations of CEO personal conduct to outcomes. The case list has selection bias: probes that ended quietly with the CEO in place are rarely public. Looney's 2022 review and the Cline et al. finding that most accused executives keep their jobs both point to that bias.
- Nvidia-specific factors (Huang's stake, absence of dual-class shares, board composition, employee loyalty) were not researched here and should come from other researchers' notes.
- I found no precedent of a mega-cap CEO's relationship partner being an alleged foreign intelligence asset, so that part of the case has no base rate.
- I did not verify whether the SpaceX or Tesla boards investigated Musk after the 2024 WSJ report.
